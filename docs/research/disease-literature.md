# Disease-condition parameters for the FES model: what the literature actually supports

Compiled 2026-11-05. All abstracts were pulled from PubMed E-utilities (efetch, rettype=abstract). Full texts came from PMC: the E-utilities XML for some papers, the PMC HTML page for others.
**Read levels:** **A** = abstract only. **FT** = I read the full text (the relevant passage or table).
The ± dispersion is labelled SD or SE only where the source states which one. "unstated" means the abstract did not say.
Wherever I did not see a number in the source, the cell says NO VERIFIED DATA. I have not inferred or estimated any number.

Healthy reference that was checked again: Mogyoros, Kiernan & Burke 1996, *Brain* 119:439, PMID 8800939, doi:10.1093/brain/119.2.439 (A). Median nerve, n = 20 healthy volunteers, threshold tracking, Weiss fit. τSD = **459 ± 126 µs** for compound muscle (motor) potentials and 665 ± 182 µs for sensory potentials. The abstract does not say whether ± is SD or SE.

## 4. Not representable in this model (central mechanisms)

- **PD:** rigidity, bradykinesia, reduced voluntary RTD (Hammond 2017), and central fatigue or reduced voluntary activation (Martignon 2022). All of these sit upstream of the motor axon. Resting tremor is generated centrally. The model could only add it as an exogenous force or activation oscillation at about 4–6 Hz, and I have no verified amplitude to use.
- **SCI:** spasticity, clonus, spasms, H-reflex changes (Lotta 1991; Schindler-Ivens 2000/2004) and changes in motoneuron excitability (Thomas 2017). Spasms in the TA were "not sufficient to maintain normal muscle properties" (Stein 1992). Lotta 1991 attributes some fibre transformation to spasticity. Gorgey 2008 (title only, not read) suggests spasticity may preserve muscle size; that is unverified.
- **Both:** supraspinal recruitment order. Surface FES recruitment is set by axon geometry and thresholds, not by the size principle.

## 5. Conflicts and caveats

1. **τSD after SCI. This is the main conflict.**
   - Lin 2007 (chronic; abstract only): τSD falls to 0.13 ± 0.02 ms, against 0.43 ms in controls.
   - Lee 2015 (acute < 6 months, mixed AIS; full text): CPN τSD unchanged, 0.46 vs 0.44 ms.
   - Reconciling the two by time since injury is consistent with Lin's statement that changes progress over time. **That is an interpretation, not a measured time course.**
   - Lin's abstract does not say which nerve the τSD figure comes from (median or CPN), the time since injury, or the AIS grade. **The full text of Lin 2007 should be read before τSD = 0.13 ms is used for the soleus/tibial nerve.**
2. **Twitch speed after SCI goes in opposite directions depending on the muscle.**
   - Quadriceps gets faster: Gerrits 1999 (faster), Scott 2006 (14% faster CT, 38% faster relaxation).
   - Thenar slows: Häger-Ross 2006 and the Thomas 2014 review (PMID 23901835) report slower twitch contraction times and lower f50.
   - Soleus is fast-shifted by fibre type (Grimby 1976; Shields' program). **I found no verified baseline CT or HRT in ms for chronic paralysed soleus.** The Shields 1995 and 1997 abstracts report only changes during fatigue.
3. **Force–frequency after SCI.** The fast fibre profile suggests a rightward shift, yet the quadriceps FFR is *left*-shifted (Gerrits 1999; Scott 2006), which the authors attribute to large twitch/tetanus ratios. Single-fibre data agree with this: CSA and peak force are preserved (Malisoux 2007).
4. **Soleus FI depends on the protocol.** The healthy/acute reference (FI 0.89 ± 0.08 vs 0.19 ± 0.06) uses 120 contractions and torque_120/torque_1 (Chang & Shields 2011). Shields 2006 uses 125 trains and final/initial torque, and its "acute" bin is < 0.5 y, which gives 0.80 ± 0.03 SE. The "acute" groups are also defined differently: < 2 weeks in Chang & Shields, < 6 weeks in Shields 1995, < 0.5 y in Shields 2006.
5. **Fibre type is preserved in the first 6 months** (Castro 1999a; Burnham 1997 < 1 month), and the transition runs from about 1 to 20 months. Acute-SCI parameters should therefore keep healthy fibre composition but carry atrophy (soleus CSA −12% from 6 to 24 weeks).
6. **No healthy-control soleus FI, CT/HRT or CSA came from these SCI papers.** Chang & Shields 2011 used acute SCI as the "fatigue-resistant" reference, not able-bodied controls.
7. **PD peripheral data are weak.** The MUNE deficits are small and mostly in hand muscles: EDB was not individually significant (Caviness 2002), and the Krenovsky cohort mixed in atypical parkinsonism. The neuropathy evidence conflicts on levodopa. Vanta 2019 shows a strong L-dopa association, while Zis 2017 finds little direct evidence and points to B12/MMA/homocysteine. Neuropathy prevalence varies widely: 16.3% pooled (Zis), 49.3% (Vanta), 65.85% (Kwon). Motor NCV in PD was normal-range and showed no L-dopa effect in Vanta, but there was no healthy control group. **Defensible PD peripheral changes:** a small reduction in MU number, possible reduced CMAP when neuropathy is present (it is mainly sensory), and fibre-type grouping without a verified % shift. CT, f50, τSD and FES fatigue in PD all lack data.
8. **Population mismatch.** Gorgey 2007 (ASIA B/C) and Lee 2015 (AIS A–D) include incomplete lesions. Scott 2006 studied adolescents and young adults.
9. **Abstract-only items.** Lin 2007 τSD, Castro 1999a/b, Malisoux 2007, Modlesky 2004, Scott 2006, Gerrits 1999, Stein 1992, Krenovsky 2020, Caviness 2002, Zis 2017, Hellwig 2009 and Martignon 2022 were all read as abstracts only. In those abstracts, ± is unspecified unless the table says otherwise.
